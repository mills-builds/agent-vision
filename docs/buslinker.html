The BusLinker V3.0: how your Mac talks to a chain of servos

One little board, two cables in, one cable out. Everything below points at a part of Fig 1.

1. What the BusLinker is for

Your Mac can't drive a servo by itself: it has no servo socket and its USB port can't supply enough power. The BusLinker V3.0 sits in between. It takes commands from the Mac over USB, takes power from a 12 V brick, and puts both onto one servo cable. Hiwonder calls it a "servo debugging board". It's what LeRobot and Hiwonder's ServoStudio software talk to.

2. Two cables in, for two different jobs

USB-C (the white cable) carries commands only. The Mac sends small messages like "servo 2, go to position 3072". The small square serial chip next to the USB socket turns USB into the plain one-wire serial signal that the servos understand. On the bus this runs at 1,000,000 bits per second by default.

The 12 V barrel jack (the black cable) powers the servos. Each HX-30HM wants 9 to 12.6 V and can draw up to 3 A when it's straining, far more than USB can give. The board passes that supply straight down the bus cable to every servo. The board accepts 5 to 14 V ("MAX 14V" is printed next to the jack); your 12 V 5 A brick is the right kind.

The blue screw terminal is a second way to bring in the same power (Hiwonder suggests an 11.1 V lithium battery there, red wire to +, black to −). You don't need it when the brick is plugged in, and it's safest not to use both at once.

3. Try the toggle: 12 V unplugged

Press 12 V unplugged. The barrel plug comes out and lies on the bench, the dotted power stops, and the PWR light goes out. The Mac can still send commands over USB (the command dashes keep running), but the servos have no power, so nothing moves and nothing answers. That's the most common "it's connected but does nothing" problem: USB plugged in, 12 V forgotten. Plug it back in and the next command works.

4. The bus: one cable, many servos

A normal hobby servo needs its own wire back to the controller. These are bus servos: they share one cable. The bus cable has three wires (−, + and S on the board's silkscreen): ground, 12 V power and the signal. Each servo has two sockets, so the cable goes board → servo 1, then a short cable servo 1 → servo 2, then servo 2 → servo 3. That's a daisy chain. Every servo is wired to the same three wires, so every servo hears every message.

5. Each servo answers only to its own ID

If they all hear everything, how does only one move? Every message starts with an ID number. Each servo stores its own ID (1 to 253) and ignores messages for other IDs. In the figure the white label on each servo has dots for its ID: one dot is ID 1, two dots ID 2, three dots ID 3. Press Send to ID 3: the command dashes go to all three servos, but only servo 3's horn turns. Then the Mac asks ID 3 where it is, and only servo 3 sends the blue reply back with its position, so the Mac knows where it actually got to.

Watch out: every HX-30HM ships set to ID 1. Two servos with the same ID on one bus will both obey and both try to reply at once, which garbles the reply. So plug in one servo at a time and give each its own ID before you chain them (your plan uses 1 to 5 for the arm's joints).

6. Positions, not angles

The servos count one full turn as 0 to 4095. So 0 is 0°, 1024 is 90°, 2048 is 180° and 4095 is just under 360°. The readout above shows both. A command is just "ID, go to this number".

7. The other parts on the board

  • Three servo ports along one edge (the back edge in the figure, where the bus cable leaves), all wired to the same bus, just different plug sizes: 5264 (the biggest, used by your HX-30HM cables), PH2.0 and 1.25T. Use whichever fits your servo's plug; they're not three separate buses.
  • The jumper (the little black cap on the pins) chooses who drives the bus. On the Servo + USB pins the Mac is in charge over USB-C, which is what you want. On Servo + TTL an external microcontroller (an Arduino, ESP32 or Raspberry Pi) would drive it instead.
  • The three-pin header (GND, T, R) beside the LEDs is that TTL serial connection for an external microcontroller. You won't need it with the Mac.
  • Three LEDs: PWR (power), TX (sending) and RX (receiving). In the figure TX glows while a command goes out, RX when a reply comes back, PWR while 12 V is in.
  • Four mounting holes, 36 × 28 mm apart, for screwing the board onto standoffs, as drawn.

What I couldn't verify (check before relying on it)

  • Which port is which. Hiwonder lists 5264, PH2.0 and 1.25T ports. I matched them to the three white ports on your board by size (largest = 5264, at the jack's end of that edge; smallest = 1.25T, at the LED end). I couldn't read a label on the board confirming it.
  • Pin order − + S is read from the silkscreen beside each port in your photo, with S toward the LED end. The pins themselves can't be seen in the photo, so I haven't checked them against the cable.
  • The jumper's pin layout is drawn as three pins (TTL, Ser, USB) with the cap on Ser + USB. Hiwonder only says "put the cap on Servo and USB"; the real block may be a 2 × 2 or 2 × 3 group.
  • LED behaviour: the silkscreen says TX, RX, PWR. Whether PWR lights from USB alone, from 12 V, or both, and exactly when TX/RX blink, isn't documented; the figure assumes PWR = 12 V present.
  • Serial chip: Hiwonder's guide installs a CH341 driver, so it's probably a WCH CH34x-family chip; the marking on your board wasn't legible. Recent macOS versions usually include a driver for these, but check that the board shows up as a serial port.
  • Servo sockets: the HX-30HM uses 5264-3P connectors; that each servo has two sockets (in and out), and where they sit on the case, is drawn from typical Hiwonder bus servos, not checked on yours. Your servos in the photo have bare brass splines; the round horn is drawn so you can see them turn.
  • "Unplugged" view: with only USB connected, I've assumed the serial chip still sends (it's powered by USB) but the servos are dead. Hiwonder doesn't say whether any USB power reaches the bus.
  • Plug fit: the board's jack takes a 5.5 × 2.1 mm plug (Hiwonder). Your plan notes a possible mismatch with the brick's plug; check the brick's plug size before forcing it.
  • Replies: on Hiwonder's bus protocol a servo answers when it is asked something ("where are you?"); a plain move command may get no answer at all. The figure assumes the Mac reads the position back after each move, as servo software usually does. I haven't checked this on the HX-30HM.
  • Labels: on your servos the white label sits on the top, next to the spline. The figure puts it on the front so the ID dots can be seen.
  • Traces, small parts, the regulator and capacitor near the jack are illustrative. The "Hiwonder V3.0" marking is moved to the middle of the board so you can read it; on your board it is printed beside the jack, which hides it from this view.
