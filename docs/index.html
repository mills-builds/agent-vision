1. What you are looking at

This is a small robot arm used as a camera arm. It is the SO-ARM101 "follower" arm, but with no gripper. Where the gripper would be, a printed camera plate carries a small USB camera (an Arducam UC-A55). The arm stands on a 292 mm printed riser made of two sections, and two G-clamps hold the riser's foot to the back edge of the bench.

The top section is printed upside down (its flat top plate on the print bed). Turn it over to build the riser: its short square peg drops into the socket on top of the bottom section, the two flanges meet at 150 mm, and the top plate ends up on top at 292 mm. The arm's base bolts onto that plate.

Five motors move it. Each one is a bus servo (Hiwonder HX-30HM) with its own ID number, from 1 at the base to 5 at the wrist. A laptop sends the commands. The thin lines from the camera show what it sees: the outline on the bench is its footprint, and it moves when you move a joint.

2. What a servo is, and what is inside

A servo is a motor that goes to a position you ask for and then holds it. A plain motor just spins. A servo turns to "here" and stops, and pushes back if something tries to move it. Press Inside a servo (or S) to open one. It has four main parts:

  • A small DC motor. It spins fast but has little force.
  • A gear train. Small gears drive big gears, stage after stage. Each stage turns slower and pushes harder. In the HX-30HM the motor turns about 345 times for one turn of the output (1:345 [V]). That is why a small motor can lift an arm.
  • A position sensor. A tiny magnet is glued to the bottom of the output shaft. Right under it, a 12-bit magnetic encoder chip reads the magnet's angle as a number from 0 to 4095 (4096 steps per turn).
  • A control board. Its little computer listens on the bus. It compares "where I am" (from the sensor) with "where I was told to go" and drives the motor until the two match. Then it holds there.

In the open servo, the motor, the sensor chip and the controller light up while the servo is working, and the gears turn at their real ratios (the model uses four stages of about 1:167; the real one is 1:345). The inside model is a generic bus servo, not a teardown of the HX-30HM.

3. How a joint turns

Every joint is built the same way. The servo's case is bolted to one link of the arm. Its horn (the round plate on the output spline) is bolted to the next link. When the output turns, the next link turns with it, around the output shaft. That shaft is the joint's axis.

IDJointThe case sits in…The horn turns…
1Base yawthe base on the riserthe shoulder bracket, left and right
2Shoulderthe shoulder bracketthe upper arm, forward and back
3Elbowthe top of the upper armthe forearm
4Wrist pitchthe end of the forearmthe wrist, tilting the camera up and down
5Wrist rollthe wristthe camera plate, spinning the picture

So each servo rides on the link before it. Servo 5 rides on servo 4, which rides on servo 3, and so on. Moving the shoulder carries the elbow, the wrist and the camera with it.

4. How a command gets to one servo

Follow the light in the figure when you move a slider.

  1. The laptop sends a short message over USB, like "ID 3, go to 2560".
  2. The BusLinker board on the riser foot turns USB into the servos' one-wire serial signal (1,000,000 bits per second by default). Its TX light blinks.
  3. The signal goes onto the bus cable. It runs from the board to servo 1, then servo 1 to servo 2, and on up the arm. This is a daisy chain. Every servo is on the same three wires (ground, 12 V and signal).
  4. So every servo hears every message. Each one checks the ID at the start. Only the servo with the matching ID moves. The others ignore it.
  5. When the laptop asks, that same servo replies with where it got to. In the figure a dimmer pulse runs back to the laptop, and the BusLinker's RX light blinks.

Two cables, two jobs. The 12 V 5 A brick powers the servos through the BusLinker and the bus cable. USB carries commands only. With USB in and the 12 V brick unplugged, messages still go out, but nothing moves and nothing answers.

Set the IDs first. Every new servo ships as ID 1. Two servos with the same ID both obey, and their replies collide. So plug in one servo at a time, give it its ID (1 base, 2 shoulder, 3 elbow, 4 wrist pitch, 5 wrist roll), and only then chain them.

Positions, not angles. A command is just "ID, go to this number". The servo counts one full turn as 0 to 4095: 1024 is 90°, 2048 is 180°, 3072 is 270°. Each slider shows that number, the same number as a dial angle, and (in brackets) how far the joint is from its straight, zero position. The readout reads like id 3 elbow → 2560 · 225°.

5. How five joints aim one camera

  • Base yaw (1) swings the whole arm left and right.
  • Shoulder (2) and elbow (3) work as a pair. Together they set how far out the camera is and how high.
  • Wrist pitch (4) tilts the camera: straight down, forward, or anywhere between.
  • Wrist roll (5) spins the camera around its own lens. It does not change where the camera points, only which way is "up" in the picture. Use it to square the picture with the bench edge. After a base swing, roll the wrist back by the same angle.

Try the presets. Overhead puts the lens about 390 mm above the bench, looking straight down. The camera's wide lens (95° × 76° [V]) then sees a patch about 840 × 600 mm. A rule of thumb: from height h it sees about 2.2h × 1.6h. Go higher to see more, lower to see detail. Look forward leans the arm out and tilts the camera 60° down at the front of the bench. Its far edge runs past the front of the bench, so the footprint stops at the edge. Home folds the arm up, with the camera looking down near the riser. Its wide view runs off the sides of this 900 mm bench, so there too the footprint stops at the edges.

Speed. At 11.1 V an HX-30HM turns 60° in about 0.19 s [V], which is about 316° per second. In the figure every joint eases to its target at that speed or slower.

Soft limits. The sliders stop at the joint limits in the arm's URDF file. The figure also refuses a move that would bring the arm or camera close to the riser, its base or the bench. When that happens it stops short and says "stopped: would hit the riser". From Home the arm is folded tight, so the shoulder can only lift a little before the camera would reach the base: press a preset, or open the elbow first. Real arm software needs the same kind of check.

Assumptions and accuracy flags

Labels: [M] measured or read from the build files, [V] vendor spec (not measured), [A] assumption, check at the bench.

  • Arm meshes [V/A]. The arm is drawn from TheRobotStudio's SO-101 files and URDF. Those are made for Feetech STS3215 servos. The Hiwonder SO-ARM101 parts that get printed are similar but differ slightly (different servo pockets), so small shapes and offsets will not match exactly.
  • Servo mesh is a stand-in [A]. The servo cases are the STS3215 model. It has the same envelope as the HX-30HM (24.7 × 45.2 × 35 mm [V]), but the details, horn and connector positions are not Hiwonder's.
  • Link lengths and reach [A]. Joint positions and limits come from the URDF (upper arm about 113 mm, forearm about 135 mm). They have not been measured on the built arm. Camera heights, footprints and clearances on this page follow from them.
  • Servo zero [A]. The page treats 2048 as each joint's zero (the URDF's zero pose, upper arm straight up). On the real arm, calibration sets that offset, so real numbers will differ.
  • Riser and camera plate [M, unprinted]. These are the designed CAD parts. The riser is two 150 mm sections; the top one is modelled upside down for printing and is drawn here turned over, its 60 × 40 × 8 mm peg in the bottom section's 60.5 × 40.5 mm socket, so the stack is 150 + 150 − 8 = 292 mm. The camera plate is 42 × 42 × 4 mm with four 6 mm standoffs on a 28 × 28 mm pattern (Ø2.4 mm holes, for the UC-A55's M2 holes [V]). None of it is printed or test-fitted yet.
  • Base bolt pattern [M from the CAD files, not checked on a printed part]. The build plan does not give the SO-ARM101 base's hole pattern (it says to measure it). Measured from the STL files instead: the riser's top plate has eight Ø5.5 mm holes. Four of them form two pairs, 55.6 mm apart at the front and 63.5 mm apart at the back, 69.8 mm front to back. Those match the four holes in the bottom of TheRobotStudio's base (base_so101_v2) to within 0.01 mm once the top section is turned over, and the base is placed on them, which also centres it on the plate. The other four holes (a 90 × 64 mm rectangle) line up with the bolt holes in the two flanges below and are not used by this base. The base's holes measure about 5.0 × 6.1 mm against the plate's 5.5 mm, so check the bolt size (M4 fits both). Hiwonder's printed base may differ from TheRobotStudio's: check it against the plate before printing.
  • Horn thickness [A]. The camera plate is drawn 6 mm below the wrist servo's output, as if on a 6 mm horn. The wrist bracket has a lip 4.7 mm below the output, inside the circle the spinning plate sweeps, so a thinner horn would let the plate's corners hit it. In this model the gap is 1.3 mm. Check the real horn and bracket, or add a spacer.
  • Camera [A]. The UC-A55 is drawn as a 38 × 38 mm board with a simple lens. Its field of view (95° × 76°) is the vendor figure [V]; the footprint ignores lens distortion.
  • Bench layout [A]. A 900 × 640 mm bench, riser centred on the back edge, laptop and 12 V brick placed for clarity. Your bench will differ.
  • Servo internals are generic [A]. Motor, gear layout, magnet and sensor positions show how a typical magnetic-encoder bus servo works. Only the 1:345 ratio and the 12-bit encoder are HX-30HM figures [V].
  • Bus details [V/A]. The ID-and-reply behaviour, 1 Mbps and the 0–4095 range follow Hiwonder's description of its bus servos. Whether a plain move command gets a reply, or only a "where are you?" question, is not checked on the HX-30HM.
  • Cables [A]. The bus cable and USB routes are illustrative. The bus lead is drawn running up behind the riser, clear of its flanges and top plate, and into the base through the round hole in the base's back wall to servo 1's socket. The real kit's bus leads are short (200 mm max), so you may need a longer lead from the BusLinker up the riser. The camera's own USB cable is not drawn.
  • Power plug fit [A]. The BusLinker's jack takes a 5.5 × 2.1 mm plug (Hiwonder). The plan notes a possible mismatch with the brick's plug (5.5 × 2.5 vs 4.0 × 1.7). Check the plug before forcing it.
  • Soft limits [A]. The collision check uses simple shapes. It was tested against the real meshes on 3000 random poses: no pose it accepted touched anything, but the closest came within about 1 mm (checking every 7th mesh vertex). Treat it as a model, not a guarantee, and keep real soft limits wider.
